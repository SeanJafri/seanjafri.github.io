001 / INTRODUCTION

I design and build robotic test fixtures and automated validation systems for HP Inc.'s Commercial Systems group — the machines that test the laptops and docks you may already use.

Over eight years on a small, in-house validation team, I've owned the mechanical design of fourteen+ fixtures: from a 120-solenoid keyboard actuator that types every key on a laptop, to a 12-actuator dock-cycler that plugs and unplugs USB-C, USB-A, HDMI, and DisplayPort cables thousands of times. The fourth generation of our laptop test fixture cost 87% less than the original ($3,500 → $450), with setup time cut from ten minutes to two and double the deployment density.

I work end-to-end: concept sketch, SolidWorks CAD with GD&T, MJF/SLA/FDM prototyping on HP's own Multi Jet Fusion 4200, vendor-supplied CNC parts, in-house manual machining, electronics integration (Eagle PCB layout, Arduino, servos, steppers, linear actuators), and direct factory-floor deployment with our test technicians.

I collaborate across HP's commercial-PC ecosystem — partnering with teams in Spring, TX (my home office), Fort Collins, CO, the Taipei TDC team, and the ODMs that depend on the test hardware I deploy.

What I love about this work is the tight loop between a sketch on the whiteboard and a machine that actually does the thing — a week later, the part is on the fixture and the cycle is running. After eight years at HP, I've watched our test lab evolve from a few benches into a roomful of automated systems I helped design. I'm at my best when I'm the engineer who can sit with a frustrated technician on the lab floor, take the broken fixture apart, and rebuild it before the end of the shift — and I want my next role to keep that hands-on loop intact, ideally on robots that are doing more than testing laptops.

005 / PROCESS & OPERATIONS

How I work, beyond the bench

01

Documentation & Media

Every fixture I design ships with a complete written user guide and a step-by-step video walkthrough. I treat documentation as a first-class deliverable — the technician on the lab floor has to be able to operate, maintain, and troubleshoot the hardware without coming back to me.

I'm also the team's photographer and videographer by choice. The photos and videos you see across this portfolio — and the ones the team uses in internal presentations, training, and stakeholder reviews — I shoot and edit personally.

02

Cross-Site Collaboration

My home office is HP's Spring, TX site, but the hardware I build gets used across a broader ecosystem. I attend meetings and partner with engineering teams in Fort Collins, CO and the Taipei TDC team, plus the ODMs that depend on the test fixtures running reliably in their facilities.

Working across time zones, manufacturing partners, and language boundaries has shaped how I design — clearer documentation, more standardized parts, and fewer fixture variants that require shipping a different SKU to each site.

03

Manufacturing Operations

I own the manufacturing side of the work I design. That means Bill of Materials creation and maintenance, ordering parts from vendors, and managing inventory across the lab.

When I joined the team the parts inventory was effectively a pile. I restructured the storage system end-to-end: every item now has a part number, labeled bin, and a clear association with the assemblies that use it. The technicians on the floor can now find what they need in seconds instead of minutes.
